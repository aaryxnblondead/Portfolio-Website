e:Tcab,<p>The problem: Defense attorneys in Mumbai handle bail applications immediately following the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita transition, but the new code's section-by-section mapping is scattered across three PDFs and a poorly OCR'd government portal. Lawyers were manually cross-referencing IPC sections to find their BNS equivalents, spending hours per case.</p>
<p>The constraint: The model had to work in Hindi, English, and regional languages like Marathi and Gujarati, all from a single corpus. Legal text is dense, with frequent abbreviations and table-based layouts that defeat standard OCR pipelines.</p>
<p>What I built: VidhaanAI ingests the government PDFs through Tesseract OCR with custom preprocessing, using morphological boundary detection to split compound words, followed by a LangChain pipeline that indexes sections into a ChromaDB vector store using a legal-embedding model fine-tuned on Indian case law. A Gemini-powered query layer translates natural-language bail questions into precise section lookups, then returns the IPC-to-BNS mapping with inline source citations.</p>
<p>The architecture uses a hybrid retrieval pattern: dense vector search for semantic similarity on clause text, combined with keyword filtering on section numbers and a re-ranking step that prioritizes the most recently published state amendments.</p>
<p>One tradeoff worth noting: I chose Gemini's flash model over the pro variant to keep latency under two seconds per query, which slightly increased false positives in edge cases involving overlapping jurisdiction clauses.</p>

<p>What broke: The OCR preprocessing pipeline initially treated section headers as body text because the government PDFs use bold italics inconsistently, which caused the vector store to misclassify section boundaries in 12% of cases. The fix required retraining the heading detector on a hand-labeled dataset of 400 sections from three states' legal codes.</p>
<p>The second failure was in the re-ranking logic: I weighted recency too heavily, so older but still-relevant sections were being deprioritized. That produced correct-but-stale answers that lawyers caught during the pilot. Rolling back the recency bias and adding a citation-confirmation step fixed it, but cost three weeks of iteration.</p>
<p>What I'd do differently: Build the heading detector earlier. The OCR quality was a foundational assumption, and I spent too long downstream working around poor segment boundaries instead of fixing the upstream data.</p>
